Something Before the Ship

In 1574 an English mathematician named William Bourne published a navigation manual called A Regiment for the Sea, and in its fourteenth chapter he described a way of telling how fast a ship was going. You tied a line to a piece of wood, threw the wood off the stern, and let the line run out as fast as the ship pulled away from it. For the timing you had, in his spelling, "either a minute of an houre glasse, or else a knowne part of an houre by some number of wordes, or suche other like." When the sand was out, or the words were spoken, "they hale in the logge or piece of wood againe, and looke how many fadome the shippe hath gone in that time." Then you multiplied up to an hour. That is the whole method. The wood is the log. A ship's log, the book, is named after a plank on a string, by way of the board the results were chalked on; the dictionary has "log-line" by 1613 and "log-board" by 1669, and the book came after the board.

Notice what Bourne counts: fathoms. There are no knots in his line. Somebody, in the fifty years after him, had the idea of tying marks into the line at a fixed spacing so that the sailor paying it out could count marks instead of measuring rope afterward, and if you chose the spacing right, the count was the speed. The arithmetic is a proportion. Half a minute is the hundred-and-twentieth part of an hour, so if each knot is the hundred-and-twentieth part of a mile, then the number of knots that run out in a thirty-second glass is the number of miles the ship makes in an hour. The historian D. W. Waters found "knot" being used to mean a sea mile by 1625. The mile in question was the one everybody used, the old Roman mile of five thousand feet, and five thousand divided by a hundred and twenty is forty-one and two-thirds, so the knots went in at forty-two feet. A sailor who had never heard of a proportion could stand at the taffrail and read his speed off a rope. The word "knot," as a unit, is that rope.

Here is the difficulty, and it is not the one you would guess. The sea mile was supposed to be a minute of latitude, one sixtieth of a degree, because that is what a navigator needs: a mile that matches the chart's own graduations, so a day's run in miles can be laid off in minutes with a pair of dividers. But nobody in England had measured a degree. Between June 1633 and June 1635 a mathematician named Richard Norwood did it the only way available, by walking from London to York with a chain and by pacing, correcting for every bend in the road and every hill, and in 1637 he published a small book, The Sea-man's Practice, whose preface says that "there is a greater number of feet contained in a degree than the common opinion, that a thousand paces (of 5 feet) make a mile." His figure for the mile was six thousand one hundred and twenty feet. The forty-two-foot knot was about a fifth too short. Every ship in England was reading her speed a fifth too high.

And then Norwood did something that is the reason I am writing this. He changed his own number. Having measured the mile at 6,120 feet he proposed that seamen use 6,000, and his reason, in his words, was that "the ship's way is commonly more than by the log line appears to be, and every man desires to have his reckoning something before his ship, that he fall not in with a place unexpected." Read that slowly. He is saying that the instrument's error has a direction, and that the direction is the safe one. A log that over-reads your speed puts your reckoned position ahead of your true one, so when you think you are at the coast you are still short of it, and the rocks arrive after you have started looking for them rather than before. A seaman does not want an accurate log. He wants a log that lies toward caution, by a known amount, and Norwood, who had just spent two years finding the truth, shaved his answer to keep the lie.

For the next two centuries the knot chased the mile and never caught it, and the chasing is a record of how a measurement actually lives. In 1659 John Collins could still write that "our English or Italian mile by which we reckon at sea contains 1,000 paces, each pace being 5 feet." By 1715 Henry Wilson had the principle exactly and the number not at all: "It is indisputable that the length of a knot on the log line must be the 120th part of a mile, because half a minute (the period of a 30-second sand glass) is the 120th part of an hour; but the difficulties arise from the divergency of opinion on the number of yards contained in a degree." He proposed forty-eight feet seven inches, then fifty-one feet; Collins had proposed fifty. What actually changed, when something did, was not the rope. The knots stayed at forty-two feet and the glass was cut from thirty seconds to twenty-four, which makes each knot stand for a longer mile without anyone re-tying a hundred and fifty fathoms of line. Later still, when the degree was better known, the glass went to twenty-eight seconds and the knots to forty-seven feet three inches. You can check that pair: forty-seven and a quarter feet, a hundred and twenty-eight and a half times an hour, is six thousand and seventy-five feet, which is the British Admiralty's mile of 6,080 to within a tenth of a percent. The rope had converged on the Earth. But look how it got there, by two knots and two glasses, and a long argument about yards in a degree, and a standing preference for being wrong in one direction.

The preference never left. Nathaniel Bowditch's New American Practical Navigator, in its 1826 edition, gives the whole procedure in a page. The line is about a hundred and fifty fathoms, with ten fathoms of "stray line" to carry the log clear of the ship's wake before the first mark, a red flag, goes over and the officer cries "Turn" and the glass-holder answers "Done." Bowditch states the principle, works the proportion from "about 6120 feet," arrives at fifty-one feet for the half-minute glass and forty-seven and six-tenths for the twenty-eight-second one, and then says this:

These are the lengths generally recommended in books of navigation; but it may be observed, that, in many trials, it has been found that a ship will generally overrun her reckoning with a log line thus marked; and, since it is best to err on the safe side, it has been generally recommended to shorten the above measures by 3 or 4 feet.

Two hundred years after Norwood, the standard American manual computes the correct knot and then tells you to cut it short on purpose. The same page warns that the glass "is much influenced by the weather, running slower in damp weather than in dry," and tells you how to check it against a plummet on a string thirty-nine and an eighth inches long, which swings once a second; and that if you let the log turn the reel itself instead of paying the line out by hand, "the log will come home and deceive you." The instrument is described, by the people who used it, as a thing that deceives, that swells in the damp, and that ought to be biased toward the ship being behind where you think she is. None of that is in the number. All of it is in the practice.

The rope was already on its way out when Bowditch wrote. Edward Massey patented a rotating log in 1802 that was in general use by 1836; Thomas Walker's harpoon log followed in 1861 and his taffrail log in 1878; the 1911 Encyclopædia Britannica gives the chip log a few lines of history and the knot spacing as forty-seven feet three inches for a twenty-eight-second glass, or fifty feet seven for thirty seconds, with a fourteen-second glass for speeds over six knots. The mile it uses is 6,080 feet. A 1926 textbook still sets out the proportion for students, x : 6080 :: 28 : 3600, and gets 47.29 feet. By then the thing being calculated was mostly a curiosity. Then, in 1929, the First International Extraordinary Hydrographic Conference, meeting in Monaco, defined the nautical mile as exactly 1,852 metres. Not a minute of anything; a number. The United States kept its own 6,080.2-foot mile until 1954 and Britain kept 6,080 until 1970, and a hydrographer named Alton Moody, writing in 1950 while the holdouts were still holding out, ended his history of the unit this way: "to the seaman, the nautical mile is still a minute of latitude, and who can say he is wrong?"

So here is the shape of it. For three hundred and fifty years a word for a speed was tied to a rope, and the rope was re-cut, and when the rope was too much trouble the glass was re-cut instead, each time chasing a mile that was chasing the size of the Earth, which nobody had finished measuring. The definition moved; the instrument followed. And then the definition stopped moving, by treaty, at exactly the moment when there was no instrument left to follow it. The forty-seven-foot knot with the twenty-eight-second glass works out to 1,851.66 metres an hour, which is within two hundredths of a percent of the treaty value. The rope got the answer right. It just got it right as a procedure with a lean, and the treaty wrote down the answer without the lean. A readout on a yacht today says 14.0 knots. There is no plank, no sand, no one calling "Turn," and no three or four feet shaved off for safety. The word survived whole. What it used to require, two sailors and an officer and a piece of wood and a habit of erring toward the rocks being closer than you think, did not come with it.

I do not think this is a loss, exactly. An exact unit is a better thing than a biased rope; nobody is arguing for the rope. But I want to name what happened to the bias, because it did not vanish. Norwood's "something before the ship" was a safety margin carried inside the measuring instrument, where it applied itself whether or not the navigator was thinking about it. When the unit became exact, the margin had nowhere to live except in the navigator's head, as a thing to remember to add. That is a real transfer of responsibility, from a tool that could not forget to a person who can, and it happened without anybody deciding it, because it was a side effect of fixing a number. The question I am left with is how many of the exact figures I rely on used to be procedures with a known lean, and where that lean went. The figure does not say. It is exact. The lean is in some manual from 1826, under a paragraph about damp weather, waiting for someone to notice that the instrument used to know something the number does not.
